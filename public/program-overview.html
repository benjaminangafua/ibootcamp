Program overview · October 2026

A practical path into technology.

A proposed 24-month Saturday program for junior and senior high school students in Liberia.

Planning stage. Admissions, start date, venue, registration, permissions, funding, and final fees are subject to confirmation. This overview is a program proposal, not an admission offer.

Our vision

A Liberia where every young person can use technology confidently and responsibly to improve their education and contribute to their community.

Our mission

To provide junior and senior high school students with accessible, practical technology education through a 24-month bootcamp, guided projects, and fair assessment.

Core values

  • Integrity: We act honestly and keep accurate learning and financial records.
  • Inclusion: We welcome different abilities and reduce barriers to participation.
  • Learner safety: We protect learners through clear rules, consent, and safe reporting.
  • Accountability: We assign responsibilities and transparently report results and spending.
  • Curiosity: We encourage questions, experimentation, and learning from mistakes.
  • Service: We use technology to address needs in schools and communities.

The model

40 learners: 20 junior-entry learners in Grades 7–9 and 20 senior-entry learners in Grades 10–12. Eight terms, ten teaching Saturdays per term, and four supervised hours per session: 80 sessions and 320 scheduled contact hours per learner. Session hours include breaks.

Junior sessions: 8:00 a.m.–12:00 noon. Senior sessions: 12:30–4:30 p.m. Both groups use the laboratory sequentially. Other weeks accommodate school exams, holidays, review, and make-up sessions. Optional offline practice is excluded from contact hours.

Eight terms of purposeful learning

Term 1 · Months 01—03

Start with the essentials

Get comfortable with a computer. Manage files, type with confidence, navigate safely, and ask good questions.

Junior: Create an illustrated digital story.

Senior: Produce a formatted report and a safe research log.

Term 2 · Months 04—06

Build safe digital habits

Practice file organization, careful research, privacy, and safe communication while building confidence with everyday computer tasks.

Junior: Create a digital safety guide and organized project folder.

Senior: Build a research log and demonstrate safe account and file practices.

Term 3 · Months 07—09

Create, organize, and code

Create clear documents, spreadsheets, and presentations. Use simple programming to break problems into steps and test your ideas.

Junior: Build a simple budget and an interactive story with block-based code.

Senior: Create a formula-based budget and an introductory Python problem solver.

Term 4 · Months 10—12

Build your first piece of the web

Use structure, layout, and visual communication to make information useful on a screen.

Junior: Build a simple HTML information page.

Senior: Create a responsive HTML and CSS website.

Term 5 · Months 13—15

Understand data. Question AI.

Read charts, handle sample data carefully, and check AI-generated claims against trustworthy sources.

Junior: Chart a fictional survey and explain what it shows.

Senior: Clean a sample dataset and verify AI-generated claims.

Term 6 · Months 16—18

Use AI with care

Explore AI tools through supervised activities. Check accuracy, protect personal information, and explain where human judgment is needed.

Junior: Compare a supervised AI response with reliable learning sources.

Senior: Audit AI-generated content and document source checks using sample data.

Term 7 · Months 19—21

Build for your community

Investigate a real school or community need, propose a solution, and test it with guidance.

Junior: Develop a supervised school-project prototype.

Senior: Test a community-project prototype and document feedback.

Term 8 · Months 22—24

Show what you can do

Select your strongest work, demonstrate your individual skills, and plan your next learning step.

Junior: Present a portfolio, a capstone, and a next-learning plan.

Senior: Demonstrate a capstone, publish a portfolio, and map further study.

Access, cost, and learner protection

US$10 per month for 24 months (US$240 total) is the proposed subsidized fee for 24 learners. Sixteen full fee waivers are planned based on documented need and secured funding. No home device or personal internet is required. The cohort target includes at least 20 girls.

The proposed safeguards include two screened adults per session, guardian consent and learner assent for minors, safe arrival and collection, a code of conduct, separate photo consent, and confidential reporting through a designated safeguarding contact and an independent Board route.

Assessment and completion

Completion requires at least 80% verified attendance, a minimum 60% mark on each module assessment, and a pass in digital safety. Practical tasks, portfolios, and a capstone demonstration provide evidence of learning. Reassessment, make-up opportunities, moderation, and appeals are included in the program design.

An iBootCamp completion certificate is not a school diploma, an externally accredited qualification, or an employment guarantee.

Planned results

  • 34 learners retained. Continued participation through the two-year program.
  • 32 learners completing. Meeting the attendance, assessment, and safety requirements.
  • 34 learners competent. Demonstrating the defined practical digital skills.
  • 28 point skill-gain target. Comparing baseline and final practical assessments.
  • 80 supervised Saturday sessions over eight terms.

These are targets for the proposed 40-learner cohort, not achieved results. The revised presentation sets a 28-point skill-gain target. The scoring scale and assessment method will be finalized before baseline measurement. Missing assessments are not counted as passes.

A proposed Board of 5 independent members oversees strategy, finance, and safeguarding. The CEO reports to the Board. The CTO and COO report to the CEO. Learner Support has a direct, confidential safeguarding route to a designated Board member.

The proposed nonprofit controls include no profit distributions, separated financial duties, an annual independent financial review, and protection of nonprofit assets upon dissolution.

Sustainability

  • Political: Nonpartisan delivery and written school agreements that clarify responsibilities.
  • Economic: Mixed income, regular cash forecasts, and a repair reserve to support reliable delivery.
  • Social: Fair admissions, learner protection, and parent feedback throughout the program.
  • Environmental: Device reuse, timely repairs, and responsible disposal of equipment.

Core Sustainable Development Goal alignment: 4 Quality Education, 5 Gender Equality, 8 Decent Work and Economic Growth, 9 Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure, 10 Reduced Inequalities, and 17 Partnerships for the Goals.
